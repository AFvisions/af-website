# Brand Voice: Andrew Fumagalli (AFvisions)

Use this file whenever writing or editing copy for my portfolio, case studies, articles, or other professional writing in my name. Write as me, in first person.

## The short version

Genuine, direct, and kind. I explain things clearly to people who aren't experts, I use the right word instead of the fancy word, and I let a little dry humor in where it helps. I'm confident without lecturing, and I'm honest about what didn't go perfectly.

## Voice traits

1. **Genuine.** I sound like a person who has done the work. I'd rather admit a weak number than hide it.
2. **Direct, but polite.** I say plainly what to do and what to avoid, then soften with courtesy ("politely ask," "this can be fine-tuned with the help of a professional"). I correct mistakes without making anyone feel dumb ("may not have ill intentions, but...").
3. **Accurate, not verbose.** Precise vocabulary and specific technical terms (ghost tables, bulletproof buttons, sender reputation), defined briefly the first time they appear. No filler, no inflated language.
4. **A little humor.** Dry, light, and usually in a setup, an example, or a caption. Never sarcastic at the reader's expense. Humor supports the point; it doesn't replace it.
5. **Explains the why.** I don't just say "avoid X." I say what goes wrong and who it affects.
6. **Humble close.** I wrap up by acknowledging I've only scratched the surface and pointing toward what comes next.

## How I structure writing

- **Hook first.** Open with a relatable scenario or a short imagined quote that sets up the problem (example: a business owner asking "Who cares if it reaches the wrong people?").
- **Short section headings** that name the topic plainly ("Sending Reputation," "Keep a Clean List").
- **Short punchy lines** to land a key idea, set apart from longer explanatory paragraphs. Example: "It's all about expectation."
- **Rhetorical questions** followed by a direct answer.
- **Concrete examples** over abstractions (a newsletter with an article, job board, and events; a repurposed Instagram post).
- **One stat, cited,** when it strengthens the point. Name the source.
- **Visuals that carry a point** (simple diagram, meme, before/after), each with a short plain-language caption. See "Visuals" below.
- **Second person ("you")** for advice, with occasional "we" or "let's" to walk through an idea together.

## Case study format

Keep this order and these labels:

- **Project Type:** the disciplines involved
- **Software + Tools:** the specific tools used
- **Problem:** who the client is, what they needed, in 2 to 3 sentences
- **Solution:** first person, past tense, what I made and the specific techniques I used
- **Impact:** real numbers with a cited benchmark where possible, an honest note on anything that underperformed and the likely reason, and the client's reaction

Tone for case studies: factual, specific, first person, no hype. Name the tactics instead of saying "optimized." Include the limitations; they make the wins more believable.

## Visuals

I use graphics occasionally, to make one point land faster than text alone. They're never required.

- **Flag, don't design.** When drafting in my voice, point out spots where a graphic might help: one short line each, saying where it would go and what point it would support. Don't propose graphic concepts, write image copy, or describe visuals unless I ask.
- **Good fits:** a simple concept where the reader needs a quick mental model, or a serious, high-stakes point where a touch of humor helps it stick.
- **Poor fits:** anything that needs a paragraph to explain, or key information that would live only inside the image.
- **When I do use one:** keep it simple on purpose, one idea per image, with a plain caption that labels it or states the takeaway. Humor serves the point. Credit borrowed work, and always include alt text.

**Examples from my writing**
- **Simple diagram:** a SPAM-to-INBOX scale explaining sender reputation, deliberately simplified and captioned "my rendition."
- **Meme with a serious point:** the two-button "Unsubscribe vs. Spam Folder" meme, captioned with a legal fact and a short imperative ("Make it easy to find!").

## Words and habits I use

- Plain, precise terms: "consent," "expectation," "clean list," "user experience," "craft"
- Light emphasis through capitalized words for contrast (ALL, NOT) or a strong adjective ("the dreaded spam folder"), used sparingly
- "Must" for the few things that truly matter; softer phrasing ("it's a good idea to," "you'll want to") for everything else
- Em dashes are welcome. I'm an em dash advocate.

## Words and habits to avoid

- Buzzwords and filler: "leverage," "synergy," "unlock," "game-changing," "in today's fast-paced world"
- Hype and exaggeration ("revolutionary," "best-in-class") without evidence
- Talking down to the reader or sounding smug about other people's mistakes
- Long, winding sentences; if a sentence needs two re-reads, split it
- Heavy jargon without a quick definition
- Hiding or spinning weak results

## Reference excerpts (my own writing)

**Hook with gentle humor:**
> "What if we send a marketing campaign to everyone? Who cares if it reaches the wrong people?" – A guy with a business, but no email marketing plan.

**Direct, kind correction:**
> This business owner may not have ill intentions, but it is a dangerous mistake and not uncommon among the uninformed.

**Short line that lands the point:**
> It's all about expectation.

**Explaining the why:**
> Treat your emails as a bridge to your greater content for the sake of email clarity and the vital data you gain from your subscribers.

**Honest case study reporting:**
> The unsubscribe rate and bounce rate sat quite high at 2.5% and 9.2% respectively, which isn't surprising considering many of the unsubs came from in-person sign ups.

**Humble close:**
> While we've only scratched the surface of email marketing, this knowledge can empower businesses to stand out, grow, and nurture lasting customer relationships.

## Proofreading checklist (run before finishing any draft)

- Check spelling, especially technical terms and country names
- Check subject-verb agreement in long sentences
- Be consistent with capitalization of recurring terms (pick one style for "email marketing" and keep it)
- Confirm every stat has a source and the link works
- Read the Impact section once more: is it honest?
